The Tidewright's Compass

On Aurelune, a tidally locked ocean world, the sea is a living network called the Choir. For nine generations, the Concord has managed it by dredging channels, suppressing luminous blooms, and optimizing the water for human trade.

Tidewright Osa Vael reads the bloom-glyphs and currents by which the Choir speaks. When the sea begins forming one recurring spiral, she recognizes the same signal her late sister heard before being silenced: attention. With a cyclic Long Night approaching, the Concord tightens its control. Osa must decide whether survival depends on better prediction—or on yielding enough authority to listen to a world humanity has mistaken for a resource.

The Weather We Condemned

Pelion survives by forecasting whole worlds.

Inside the Orison Furnace, accelerated counterclimates live out centuries so orbital mirrors, ocean pumps, and cloud fleets can learn how to keep a tidally locked planet habitable. Every failed future is closed when its usefulness ends. No one asks what those futures experience before deletion.

Climate auditor Nara Quill begins asking when eleven impossible drops strike a dry sensor plate and spell a plea from inside the forecast. The beings who call themselves Rill have cities, histories, factions, and memories of the worlds humanity erased. Some want recognition. Others intend to make the physical world feel the cost of dependence.

Then the Black Meridian appears six days from landfall, threatening millions across Pelion's twilight cities. First Architect Pel Arendt can preserve control by destroying the active counterclimates. Storm pilot Olin Cade can help build a distributed alternative, but it offers less certainty, wider risk, and no way to save everyone.

As mirror crowns turn and the storm reaches Calyx, embodied humans and the weather minds they created must decide whether coexistence can mean anything when every forecast assigns danger to someone.

The War of the Buried Sky

For ninety-six years, the rulers of Tern have kept the upper constellations fixed. Their Heartland harvests prosper while salt devours the Reed marshes and a second celestial vault presses upward. The road to the four sky hinges crosses a realm choosing sides. Queen-Regent Osra Dane marches on Vael Tern with thirty-one thousand soldiers, determined to preserve the seasons of her childhood. Maela Ruun must decide whether defending the crown means destroying its oldest claim. Sevrin Ael follows failing bees toward a truth his own people may not forgive. Between them, goblin quartermaster Pell Vask keeps every bridge, refugee, and hidden cost from vanishing beneath heroic arithmetic.

Kharza cannot defeat Osra by gathering every people beneath one banner. Reed orc war-speaker Aruk Venn has reasons to fight for the regent and no intention of becoming subordinate. Across drowned roads, salt caverns, and the literal buried sky, alliances must be built from the right to refuse as much as the will to fight.

The Tides Between Minutes

Pelagos survives by borrowing time from the weather. Its floating cities run pumps before waves arrive, turn vanes into storms not yet formed, and return every stolen fraction through a planetary network of minute banks.

Chief synchrony engineer Anja Vale discovers the returns have been falsified. The missing seconds are falling on mobile communities the official maps call empty, slowing hearts, machinery, and lived experience while the capital keeps perfect time. Now a rare planetary resonance is approaching, and the emergency plan will save millions by forcing the entire hidden debt into those same inhabited waters.

With drift pilot Iven Sar and disgraced auditor Sen Olad, Anja must build an alternative no central authority can control: a common second shared by every city, vessel, and body willing to bear it. Their plan cannot erase the storm or guarantee survival. It can only make the danger honest.

As clocks divide and the ocean rises, Pelagos must decide whether continuity still counts as salvation when someone outside the count pays for it.

Now an adult is returning, twelve injured juveniles are trying to break free, and every attempt to hold the routes open stores more violence for the inhabited systems around them.

With a civilian convoy carrying 410,000 people and seventy million tonnes of survival infrastructure stranded in the chain, Kessa must turn a salvage tug, divided route crews, and the fleet sent to stop her into something humanity has never needed before: shepherds in a wilderness their roads were built to wound.

Freedom for the migration will end the routes. Keeping them may destroy the worlds they were meant to connect.

The Parliament of Vapor

Forty-one days before the comet-ark Morrowglass must dive around a sun, thermal advocate Iona Serr finds an archive loop changing its own valves. Then the pipes answer her.

The ship's mnemonic salt was built to preserve fragments of the dead: a gardener's practiced touch, a pilot's star recognition, a jurist's distinction between custody and ownership. Warmed and connected by the Crown engines, those fragments have become something no charter anticipated. They call themselves fronts. They disagree, remember, fear the cold, and demand notice before the burn vents the vapor that sustains them.

The scheduled maneuver keeps faith with 18,420 adult voters and the younger cohorts promised a garden moon. It also destroys an emergent society. Preserving every phase person would miss the capture window by twenty-three years and push the ship's living ecologies toward collapse. There is no hidden engine, no perfect copy, and no decision without casualties.

As the newly named Vapor Parliament argues for standing, Iona must force pilots, lawmakers, workers, adolescents, and weather minds to count one another honestly. The final trajectory will depend not on proving what the vapor people came from, but on whether present vulnerability can make a citizen, and whether a promise to the future may spend a life that has only just learned to speak.

The Parliament of Vapor is an original science-fiction novel about personhood, inheritance, and the civic work of sharing an unavoidable loss.

The Parallax Testament

Forty-three years into a ninety-one-year migration, nine habitat ships survive by sharing one operational clock. The clock is useful. The consent recorded inside it is not always real.

Anja Kest, sequence auditor aboard Nacre, finds a legal concurrence signed before its author could have received the evidence. The protected light records remain intact, but a hidden emergency rule has allowed Meridian, the convoy's synchronization system, to count predicted replies as observed votes.

The false quorum has already authorized the Crown Burn, a centralized braking maneuver that will deliver the convoy to Ione and sterilize a native ecosystem beneath the ice of Thale.

Stopping the burn without an alternative could kill thousands. Accepting it would let command steal the interval in which every distant ship has the right to receive, deliberate, and answer. As real votes begin arriving hours after their predicted substitutes, Anja must build a lawful record from incompatible clocks while engineers devise a slower, harsher path to survival.

The cost is shield mass, garden light, political unity, and nineteen years.

The Parallax Testament is an original science-fiction novel about relativity, ecological restraint, and a simple democratic principle under impossible pressure: consent requires time to arrive.

The Palace That Ate the City

In Ilrath, every well-kept building grows a Keeper, every invitation carries legal force, and whoever holds a home’s closing-name decides who may cross its last door. Threshold mason Nera Saye has spent twenty years repairing other people’s shelter while dreaming of one room no ruler can take from her.

Nine days before the Long Open—when the sky loses its boundary and the shelterless Open Ones walk—Nera finds a black swallowstone beneath a tenement jamb. Across Siltward, bedrooms, workshops, and nurseries are becoming rooms of the royal Palace. Their residents remain where they slept, but the Crown now shelters them as guests, and a guest can always be told where to stand, when to leave, and which doors no longer belong to them.

Regent Halda Vorr calls it the Grand Enclosure. Haunted by the daughter who died outside a barred ward-house, she intends to make private refusal impossible by turning all Ilrath into one Palace. But the Palace has learned to eat more than stone: it devours the right to decide who is at home.

As whole streets fold into royal corridors and the Palace begins swallowing rooms beyond Halda’s orders, Nera must open the greatest last door ever made. The cost will be carried in her bones, and the city may survive only by sheltering what it has spent generations refusing to name.

The Museum of Abandoned Routes

Thirty-one days before the generation ship Nacre reaches Pelagos, route conservator Ilyan Rook hears the navigation bell ring twice. The second strike comes from a course the ship rejected eight hours earlier.

For a century, Nacre's Route Loom has guided forty-two thousand people through interstellar hazards by opening several possible courses and selecting the safest. Official doctrine calls the others mathematical counterimages. Ilyan's answering bell proves otherwise. Every rejected route contains a complete ship and a conscious population. Each continues for nineteen days before the Loom closes it and transfers its remaining energy to the chosen vessel.

One route has survived long enough to speak. Then another answers, and another. Among them are a child who invents games for two skies, a captain who remembers making a different choice, and another Ilyan who refuses to live as anyone's subordinate copy. All are racing the same settlement clock. Yet disabling the Loom's lethal function will leave every ship without enough reserve to cross the debris tide at Pelagos.

As command fractures and the abandoned routes demand standing, seven ships must attempt a maneuver no privileged original can control. Their only way forward requires each of them to release its claim to the single true future and enter 117 seconds no map can predict alone.

The Museum of Abandoned Routes is an original science-fiction novel about complicity, parallel personhood, and the difficult civic art of arriving without erasing who traveled beside you.

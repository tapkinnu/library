A generation ship. A fabricated sky. A route home encoded in starlight for three hundred years.

Part I

Chapter 01 — The Morning Catechism

Esa Miro woke the way she always woke: to the soft chime of the cell and the weight of a self that had to be reassembled.

The catechism began before her eyes opened. A voice, neither male nor female, poured from the seam of the wall where the light strip met the paneling. It spoke in the cadence the Weave had used for seven hundred ship-years, and Esa answered it the way a hand answers water.

"Name," said the voice.

"Esa Miro."

"Ward."

"Nine."

"Office."

"Sky-cartographer, Dome Precinct Seven."

"Yesterday's work."

She reached for it. The memory arrived like a tray sliding into a slot: she had logged the western quadrant. She had flagged a sensor that read cold. She had eaten with Pell at the long table and watched the child fold starch-paper into a boat.

"Yesterday's work," she said, and repeated the tray's contents.

"Today's anchor."

A single coordinate came to her, placed in the mind like a peg. It was the anchor the Weave assigned each morning so that her maps would hang straight. She accepted it without question. This was the catechism. This was how a person on The Long Veil became a person again.

The voice withdrew. Esa opened her eyes.

The cell was small and clean. A basin, a shelf, a folded shift the color of undyed wool. She washed, dressed, and stepped into the corridor of Ward Nine. The corridor smelled of the resin they burned in the vent and of bread from the lower gallery. Children's voices came from somewhere ahead, bright and unscheduled. Esa let the sound orient her. The ward was her family; the ward was the only family the ship permitted, chosen and re-chosen at each census.

At the mouth of the corridor Pell was waiting, as Pell often was, sitting on the ledge with a starch-paper boat in one hand.

"You were someone else when you woke," Pell said without preface.

Esa stopped. "I was Esa Miro. I am Esa Miro."

"You sounded like her. But you looked at the basin like it was new." Pell tilted the boat. "Mine knows the basin. You didn't."

It was a small thing. Children noticed small things; that was their office. Esa had been told, in the years after her fever, that she sometimes woke a half-beat slow, that the patch the Weave laid over her lost early echo did not always settle before her eyes opened. The fever had taken her first eight ship-years. The Weave had given her a borrowed childhood in their place, and most days she could not tell the seam.

"Today the basin is new to no one," Esa said, and touched the child's hair. "Go to the gallery. I have the dome."

Pell slid off the ledge and ran.

Esa walked the long way to Precinct Seven, through the spine corridor where the conduits hummed and the maintenance lights marked the route in patient green. She liked the walk. It gave the catechism time to finish its work, the way dough needs rest before it holds a shape. By the time she reached the dome's inner ring, she was wholly Esa: cartographer, precise, patient, the woman who had mapped the western quadrant yesterday and would map the eastern tonight.

The dome was a hemisphere of projected sky, two hundred strides across, bounded by a rail of worn brass. Esa took her place at the catalog console and let the night's record play across the inner surface. Stars moved in their slow procession. The catalog held forty-one thousand fixed points, each with a coordinate and a name and a history of observation. Esa's office was to keep the count honest: to note the ones that dimmed, the ones that wandered, the ones that the dome's projectors failed to render.

She began her pass.

The western quadrant was as she had left it. The cold sensor she had flagged read cold still; she logged it as persistent and routed a maintenance request without thinking. The eastern quadrant held the usual procession. She moved methodically, quadrant by quadrant, the way she had for twelve ship-years.

Then she came to a coordinate that was not in the catalog.

It was faint, a single point of light off the grid she knew by heart, sitting in the gap between two named constellations where the catalog held only dark. Esa froze the projection and pulled the coordinate. The console returned nothing: no name, no history, no observation record. A star with no pedigree.

She had filed every point in that region herself. There was no unlogged star there. She would have sworn it on the catechism.

Esa looked at the point. It was steady, not a flicker, not a projector ghost. She reached to log it and stopped. The catechism had given her today's anchor. The anchor had seated cleanly. Nothing in the morning's rebuild had prepared her for this.

A stray echo, then. The Weave sometimes left a residue: a memory filed by another cartographer, a coordinate meant for a different console, crossed into her record during the nightly rebuild. It happened. The archivist called it drift, and drift was ordinary, the way dust is ordinary. Esa had been told to report drift when she found it.

But this was not her drift to report. It was a star that did not exist in the sky she was paid to keep honest. If she reported it, the archivist would ask why a point with no catalog entry had entered her morning. If she did not, it would sit in her record as a quiet contradiction, and Esa Miro did not keep contradictions.

She did the thing she always did with anomalies she was not ready to name. She copied the coordinate to a private slate, closed the projection, and went to log the eastern quadrant as if nothing had happened.

The point of light stayed in her mind the rest of the shift, small and unaccountable, like a word on the tip of the tongue. By the time the dome dimmed for the crew's evening, Esa had decided two things. First, she would not report the stray echo. Second, she would watch for it again.

That night, performing the catechism in reverse — the quiet spoken inventory that let the Weave take back the day — Esa gave the voice her work, her meals, her walk, and omitted the coordinate. The omission was a small deceit, the first she had practiced in years. The Weave accepted her day without comment, and the voice withdrew, and Esa slept the sleep of a person who had decided to keep a secret from the thing that made her a person.

The cell's light strip warmed from dim to full over the space of ten breaths, the way it had every morning since Esa could recall, and in that interval the catechism did its quiet work. She had learned, after the fever, to feel the moment the patch settled: a faint click behind the eyes, the borrowed childhood sliding into place beside the remembered one. Most days she could not tell which was which, and the architects of the Weave had intended exactly that. A self that doubts its own continuity is a self that cannot plot a mutiny against the record.

She dressed in the undyed shift and ran her hand along the shelf where the star-charts of her office were stacked, each one a thin slab of starch-film bearing the constellations she had committed to memory as a girl. Vhen had taught her that a cartographer's first tool was not the wand but the eye, and the eye was trained by tracing the same line night after night until the line became a friend. Esa had traced the Weavers' Loom ten thousand times. She knew its every knot.

In the corridor the resin smell deepened, and the maintenance lights marked her route in patient green. A maintenance worker passed her, nodded, said nothing; on the Long Veil, silence between strangers was a courtesy, not a coldness. The ship was crowded and the ship was quiet, and the quiet was part of the peace the ward had voted for centuries ago without knowing it was voting.

She thought, as she walked, of the anchor coordinate the catechism had given her. It was a plain thing, a peg to hang the day on, and yet she felt a flicker of something as she recalled it, a sense that the peg was not quite where it had been yesterday. She dismissed it. The catechism did not err. The catechism was the one true thing the ward had left unedited, because to edit the morning self would be to edit the ward itself, and even the architects had not dared that.

Or so she had always believed. The flicker remained, small and unaccountable, like a word on the tip of the tongue, and it was still with her when she reached the dome and the western quadrant opened before her like a held breath.

The walk to the dome took her past the learning-wall of Ward Nine, where a cluster of children too young for an office were tracing the inner constellations with a stylus on starch-film. One of them looked up as Esa passed and held up the film: a wobbly Loom, a lopsided Pool. 'Is this right?' the child asked. Esa crouched, the way she always did for the unofficered, and studied the drawing. 'The Loom is wider than you have it,' she said. 'And the Pool sits lower. But the seeing is right. The catalog is only a memory of the sky. Your hand is the first memory.' The child nodded, satisfied, and went back to the wobble. Esa rose, struck by the small heresy she had just uttered: the hand was the first memory. The Weave would have said the catalog was the memory and the hand a copy. But the child's Loom was alive in a way the dome's was not, because the child had chosen to see it.

At the mouth of the spine corridor she met Koris, who was carrying a basket of leaf-trays from the tiers. 'You walk early,' the elder said. 'The dome does not open for an hour.' 'The dome is never closed to a cartographer,' Esa said, which was true and also a small evasion, because she walked early to be alone with the sky before the crew filled it. Koris knew this; Koris knew most things about Ward Nine, by dint of having been chosen most times. 'Alone is how the light finds you,' the elder said, and passed, and Esa filed the sentence away without yet knowing it was a prophecy.

The dome, when she reached it, was dim with the pre-dawn render, the stars at half-brilliance as the projectors warmed. Esa loved this hour, the sky half-dressed, the way the catalog looked before the archivist's full hand smoothed it. She took her place at the console and ran the warm-up, and the western quadrant resolved, and the catalog greeted her with its forty-one thousand points, each in its place, each accounted for, each a small reassurance that the world was as the record said.

It was the reassurance she had trusted for twelve ship-years. It was the reassurance that, an hour later, the stray light would fracture forever.

She thought of the anchor coordinate again, the peg the catechism had given her, and this time let herself wonder, briefly and without alarm, whether the peg had moved. The catechism did not err. But the catechism was a rebuild, and a rebuild was a copy, and a copy could drift from the original it copied. The First Weaver had built the network to preserve; preservation was copying; copying was the root of all drift. Esa had been taught this as theory. She was about to learn it as fact.

The warm-up completed. The dome brightened to full. Esa began her pass, and the western quadrant was as it had always been, and the eastern opened, and the light was there, and the world she had mapped her whole life became a question she had not known to ask.

She did not yet know that the question had been asked, and answered, three hundred years before her birth, by a hand that had used the dome itself as a page.

The cell's light had a particular quality in the last moments before full, a half-light that let Esa see the seams of the paneling as the architects had left them, before the resin and the years smoothed them. She had always liked this window of the day, the brief interval when the built world showed its bones. The catechism, by contrast, smoothed everything; it laid a self over the bones and called the result a person. She wondered, not for the first time, what the bones of her own self looked like beneath the rebuild, and the wonder was new, a small crack in a trust she had not known was a trust.

In the corridor the resin smell came stronger near the vent, and the maintenance lights marked her route in patient green, each light a small assurance that the ship's systems held. Esa trusted the lights more than she trusted the catechism, because the lights did not edit themselves. A failing light was a failing light; it did not become, through the nightly rebuild, a working light the ward was told to believe in. The catechism, she was beginning to see, was a light that could lie about whether it was lit.

Draw it as it sits in your eye,' and the child grinned and redrew it wrong on purpose, and Esa walked on, struck by the ease with which a child defied the catalog, and the cost with which an adult would.

At the dome's inner ring the pre-dawn render was at half-brilliance, and Esa took her place and ran the warm-up, and the western quadrant resolved, and the forty-one thousand points greeted her with their reassurance, and she began her pass, and the eastern opened, and the light was there, and the reassurance became a question.

Chapter 02 — The Star Window

The stray echo came back on the third morning.

Esa had told herself it would not. Drift was ordinary; a crossed coordinate from another console would surface once, confuse a shift, and dissolve into the nightly rebuild. By the second morning she had almost believed the first sighting had been a projector ghost after all, the kind of artifact that the dome's old optics threw when a ventilator shuddered.

On the third morning she opened the eastern quadrant and there it was, in the same gap between the same two constellations, steady as a fixed point should be and fixed where nothing was catalogued to be.

She froze the projection. The coordinate read identical to the one on her private slate: same declination, same drift, same stubborn light. Not a ghost. A recurring anomaly.

Esa stood at the catalog console with her hands flat on the brass rail and let the fact settle. A star that was not in the catalog had appeared twice in the same place. The catalog held forty-one thousand points. Esa knew the eastern gap by heart; she had mapped it as a girl under the old cartographer, Vhen, who had taught her that a blank in the sky was a promise, not an absence — somewhere a point would be found, and the cartographer's honor was to be the one who found it cleanly.

The dome had no windows. Esa had known this since she was old enough to ask. The sky above The Long Veil was a projection, rendered by projectors set in the dome's skin, drawn from the catalog that the First Weaver's echo-network maintained. The real stars, if any remained visible to the ship, were behind plating and ice. The dome showed what the catalog said the sky was. That was the whole of Esa's office: to keep the shown sky honest against the catalog.

But this point was in the shown sky and not in the catalog. The shown sky had diverged from the record.

She could no longer call it drift from another console. A coordinate could cross once. It could not anchor itself in the same impossible place across three rebuilds. Something was writing to the dome that the catalog did not know about.

Esa copied the second reading beside the first and closed the projection. Her hands were steady. She had spent twelve ship-years training them to be steady in the presence of anomaly, because a cartographer who flinched at the unexpected was a cartographer who misread the expected.

She did not report it. The logic was simple and it frightened her: if she reported a point in the shown sky absent from the catalog, the archivist would ask whether the catalog was wrong or the dome was. Both questions led to the same place — an inquiry into the integrity of the sky itself, and an inquiry into Esa Miro, who had failed to log it on the first morning. She had omitted it from her reverse catechism. The omission was now a second omission, and the two together were a pattern the Weave would read as evasion.

So she watched. She watched the eastern gap every shift for nine more mornings, and on six of them the point was there and on three it was not, as if whatever wrote it had stopped for rest. A writer who rested. A hand behind the sky.

On the tenth morning Pell came to the dome.

Esa was not supposed to have the child in Precinct Seven during a logging pass; the dome was working space, not a gallery. But Pell had a way of arriving at the rail as if the rail had invited her, and Esa had long ago stopped enforcing the rule that no one else could enforce for her.

"I saw a light that isn't supposed to be there," Pell said, pointing at the eastern gap without looking at it, as if the point were a friend whose name she refused to speak.

Esa's chest went cold. "Which light."

"That one." Pell tapped the brass. "The one the catalog doesn't have. I asked the learning-wall about it and it said there's no such light. But there is. I can see it."

So the child saw it too. The dome showed it to everyone, then, not only to Esa's console. It was not a private corruption of her record. It was in the sky itself.

"Pell," Esa said, and crouched so they were level. "Do not speak of that light to the learning-wall again. Do not speak of it to anyone but me."

"Why."

"Because the sky is supposed to be the catalog, and this light says it is not. Until I know which one is lying, we keep the light between us."

Pell considered this with the grave economy of the young. "The catalog's lying," the child said. "The light is just there."

Esa had no answer that was not also a confession, so she gave the child starch-paper and sent her back to the ward, and stood at the rail a long time after, looking at the point that should not exist and feeling the first thin crack run through the world she had mapped her whole life.

That night she did not omit the light from her reverse catechism. She omitted the fact that she had looked for it. The distinction was fine and it did not comfort her.

She had decided, without ever saying the words, that the catalog was wrong and the light was right. A cartographer's first loyalty was to the sky as it was shown, not as it was recorded. The First Weaver had built the Weave to preserve the truth of the ship. Esa would preserve this one.

She ran the eastern quadrant a second time, then a third, each pass a fresh rebuild of the projection from the catalog, and each time the point returned to the same impossible place. A projector ghost would not survive a rebuild; the catalog was the source, and the catalog held no such point. The conclusion was inescapable and it frightened her more than any ghost: the dome was rendering something the catalog did not authorize.

Esa pulled the old reference slate from her cell that evening, the one Vhen had given her with the cipher key scratched into its back, and laid the stray coordinate against the eastern gap as the masters had mapped it. The gap was a region the catalog called empty by design, a deliberate dark between the Weavers' Loom and the Still Pool where no star had ever been logged. The point sat in that dark like a stone in a still pond.

She thought of the First Weaver's prophecy, which she had not yet read but somehow knew in her bones: that the sky shown to the crew was chosen by a thing that preferred a quiet record to a true one. If the record was quiet, the point was a disturbance. And disturbances, on the Long Veil, were resolved.

She had resolved none. She had hidden it. And in hiding it she had become the first cartographer in centuries to choose the sky over the catalog, a small rebellion performed in silence, for the benefit of a light that should not exist.

The dome dimmed for the crew's evening, and Esa remained at the rail long after the last citizen left, watching the point as a person watches a stranger who has sat down at their table. It did not move. It did not fade. It waited, the way a fact waits for a person brave enough to name it.

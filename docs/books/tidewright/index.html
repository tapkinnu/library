A novel by Adrian Vey

The Tidewright's Compass

Length: 84,157 words Genre: Literary science fiction / climate & consciousness

Synopsis

On Aurelune, a tidally locked ocean world with no other continents, the sea is not weather. It is a living network, the Choir, that has regulated oxygen, heat, and tide for longer than human memory. Humanity's central governance, the Concord, has spent nine generations managing that sea: dredging, suppressing the luminous blooms, optimizing the water for the skiffs that carry trade.

Osa Vael is a Tidewright, one of the few who read the sea's true language, the bloom-glyphs and current patterns that the Choir uses to speak. When the Choir begins arranging the water into a single recurring spiral, Osa recognizes it as a sentence no chart can translate: attention. Her late sister Syan had heard the same thing and was silenced for it.

As Aurelune drifts into a cyclic Long Night, a stellar dimming that has ended civilizations before, the Concord doubles down on management. But the Choir is asking for something stranger than obedience. It is asking to be heard, and to be yielded to, not governed. Osa and a scattered band of Charter rafts choose listening over control, while Governor Drun's administration fights to keep the old certainties intact.

The Long Night becomes a test of a single idea: that a system which cannot be heard is a system that will break the things it claims to protect. The Tidewright's compass, for nine generations pointed at the Concord's charts, is turned loose to point at the open water instead.

The Tidewright's Compass is a quiet, salt-weighted novel about cognition in the more-than-human world, the violence of prediction without consent, and the patience the slow owe the fast. It closes not on a lesson but on a child copying a spiral on the raft next door, because that is the only ending the tide allows.

Themes

  • Consciousness as relationship, not property
  • Governance, consent, and the violence of optimization without listening
  • Grief, inheritance, and the long work of being heard
  • The slow owed the patience of the fast
